01 · Orientation

A working draft built only on numbers Moon Drops has actually confirmed

This is the V/TO execution layer: five 12-month company goals, a cascade of 90-day Rocks for each leadership seat, and a short list of numeric decisions only the board can make. It is deliberately unfinished in the places where the business does not yet have data.

The labelling system

Confirmed Supplied by Moon Drops from business records.
Interpretation Arithmetic or inference drawn from confirmed numbers.
Proposed Advisor suggestion. No standing until the board votes.
Decision needed The board must choose a number or a definition.
Gap Data does not exist yet, or has not been supplied.

Where the handover flagged an earlier inaccuracy — notably the claim that the reported average ticket was simply "consistent" with gross sales — this draft corrects it rather than carrying it forward.

What this draft concludes

  1. The revenue conversation is not ready for a vote yet. The 2026 tasting-room year-to-date figure has no stated as-of date, so it can be annualised to anywhere between roughly $318K and $477K. That single missing detail decides whether $400K is a stretch or already behind pace.
  2. Two of the five goals should be "build the baseline," not "hit the number." Moon Drops has no confirmed profit, margin, or cash figures in the record, and no guest-advocacy measure. Setting targets on top of missing baselines is how a V/TO loses credibility in month four.
  3. The tasting-room-as-heart principle is now provable, not just felt. Tasting-room revenue per gallon runs roughly 52% higher than self-distributed revenue per gallon. That is the strongest strategic fact in the confirmed dataset and it belongs in front of the board.
  4. The proposed $400K / $200K split loads about 91% of all company growth onto one channel. That may still be the right call, but it should be a conscious choice rather than a by-product of picking round numbers.
  5. The first Rock cycle should be small and mostly foundational. Seven Rocks across six seats, weighted toward definitions, data, and holiday execution — not toward launching new programmes during the strongest selling weeks of the year.
On the Business Operations seat

03 · Reconciliation & data integrity

Four numbers that must be settled before any Rock is written

None of these are large amounts of money. All of them will quietly corrupt the scorecard for a year if they are not frozen now. Fixing definitions is cheaper before the board approves targets than after.

Item 1 — The average ticket does not tie

CalculationResultDistance from reported $39.18
Gross $198,608.00 ÷ 4,878 orders$40.72$1.54 higher — not the source
Net $191,314.79 ÷ 4,878 orders$39.22$0.04 higher — almost certainly the source
Net $191,314.79 ÷ $39.18 reported ticket4,883 ordersImplies the POS counts ~5 more orders than reported

The reported ticket is a net measure, not a gross one. The residual four cents resolves if the POS average-ticket denominator is roughly 4,883 rather than 4,878 — five transactions somewhere are being included in one report and excluded from the other. Likely candidates: zero-dollar or fully comped tickets, voided-then-reissued orders, gift-card loads counted as transactions, or a tab-split rule.

Recommendation

Adopt one MDOS definition and never revisit it: average ticket = net tasting-room revenue ÷ paid orders. Baseline it at $39.22 rather than $39.18, because $39.22 is reproducible from the confirmed inputs and $39.18 is not. Then have the POS export identified once so the two order counts agree permanently.

Net is also the right basis on business grounds: gross flatters the metric, net is what reaches the bank and flows into margin.

Item 2 — The $7,293.21 gross-to-net gap is unexplained

Gross minus net equals $7,293.21, which is 3.67% of gross revenue. Moon Drops should know precisely what that is composed of, because each possibility implies a different management action:

If the gap is mostly…Then it is…And the action is…
Discounts and promotionsA pricing decisionDecide who may discount, by how much, and track it weekly
Comps and staff/industry poursA culture and cost controlSet a comp budget as a % of revenue; make it visible
Refunds and voidsA service or training signalRoot-cause the top three reasons
Tax, tips, or fees mis-mappedA reporting errorFix the POS mapping — it is not revenue leakage at all

Item 3 — Is the 2025 figure gross or net? Gap

The 2025 tasting-room total of $329,286.35 is not labelled gross or net anywhere in the record. This matters more than it appears:

  • If 2025 is gross, then 2026 gross year-to-date of $198,608.00 represents 60.3% of the full prior year.
  • If 2025 is net, the comparable 2026 figure is $191,314.79, or 58.1% of the prior year.
  • Comparing 2026 net against 2025 gross would understate growth by roughly two percentage points — enough to make a target look met or missed at the margin.

Item 4 — "Year to date" has no as-of date Critical gap

This is the blocking item

Without the as-of date, the 2026 tasting-room run rate is indeterminate, and every revenue goal below is therefore provisional. See Scenario math for the range. This is a one-email fix and should be resolved before the board meets, not during it.

Why this table should drive the hospitality Rock

Every $1.00 added to the net average ticket is worth about $4,878 on the year-to-date order count alone, and roughly $10,000 against a full year near 10,000 orders — with no additional guest, no additional marketing dollar, and no additional parking space.

05 · Company goals

Five 12-month goals for the V/TO

Three are performance goals with numbers the board must set. Two are baseline goals, because Moon Drops cannot responsibly target a profit figure or an advocacy score it has never measured. Each goal names the pillar it serves and the seat that owns it.

A note on the shape of this list

It is tempting to make all five goals revenue-flavoured because revenue is the only thing currently measured. Resisting that is the point. A V/TO that measures only revenue trains a lean team to chase volume at the expense of margin, quality, and the guest experience — which is precisely the outcome the Moon Drops non-negotiables exist to prevent.

Why this goal is framed with a mix guardrail. A revenue number alone can be hit in a way that damages the strategy — for example by discounting hard into distribution. Attaching a mix floor means the goal cannot be achieved by turning Moon Drops into a wholesale business, which protects both the brand and the per-gallon economics.

Recommended sequencing. Approve the combined figure at the board meeting only if the as-of date and 2026 wholesale actuals are in hand. If they are not, approve a range and a date by which the point target will be set — a range the team believes beats a number the team quietly knows is fiction.

The most serious gap in the V/TO

Moon Drops is preparing to commit to a revenue number without a single confirmed profitability figure. A distillery can grow revenue 15% and still be worse off — more liquid tied up in barrels, more staffing hours, more distributor terms, more working capital. Until gross margin by channel exists, no one in the room can say whether the tasting-room dollar and the wholesale dollar are worth the same, and the entire "heart versus reach" strategy rests on assuming they are not.

What "good" looks like here. Not a full accounting overhaul. One page, monthly, showing: revenue by channel, cost of goods by channel, gross margin percentage by channel, operating expenses, operating profit, cash on hand, and thirteen weeks of projected cash. That page is the deliverable. The profit target follows once there are three months of it.

Decision needed The average-ticket definition and target, and what the Magic Metric actually is

On the Magic Metric. The concept has been accepted but the instrument has not. The lowest-friction version that still means something: a single question asked at point of sale or by follow-up text — "How likely are you to bring someone else here?" — because the brand promise is that every guest leaves with a story worth sharing, and bringing someone back is the behavioural proof of that promise. Recommendation is to instrument it and gather a baseline first; do not attach a target to it in the first cycle.

Why +5% on ticket rather than something bolder. A 5% lift is roughly $1.96 per order — a defensible ask that a trained team can achieve through flight-to-bottle attachment and better cocktail-to-carry-out conversion, without pressure selling that would undermine the hospitality culture. It is worth close to $20,000 across a year near 10,000 orders.

The constraint nobody has checked

A grain-to-glass distillery cannot sell spirits it has not already made and aged. A $600K revenue goal implies a specific volume of liquid across specific SKUs at specific times — including holiday carry-out demand that has to be barrelled, bottled, and labelled well in advance. If aging inventory cannot cover it, the revenue goal is not aggressive, it is impossible, and better to know that in the boardroom than in November.

This is also where the quality non-negotiable gets teeth. Growth pressure and quality pressure collide inside production first. Baselining yield and batch consistency now means that if quality drifts next year, it will be visible as a number rather than as an argument.

What the Give Back pillar is missing

Honest flag

We Give Back currently has no measurable baseline of any kind — no confirmed figure for community contribution, local agricultural sourcing, partnership activity, or local economic impact. Rather than invent one, the recommendation is to leave it un-targeted in the first cycle and give it a defined measure in the second. Naming this gap out loud in front of the board is better than presenting three pillars where only two are governed.

07 · 90-Day Rocks

The full Rock menu, by seat

One to three candidate Rocks per seat, each tied to a company goal, each with a single owner and a measurable done-condition. This is the menu — not the commitment. Section 08 recommends which seven of these to actually run first.

The test each Rock has to pass

Specific · measurable · owned by exactly one person · achievable in 90 days · traceable to a company goal. A Rock that cannot be scored "done / not done" at the end of the cycle is a project, not a Rock. Rocks marked Defer are good work that should wait, because a lean team wearing multiple hats cannot carry three Rocks per person and still run the business.

09 · Calendar & cadence

When the planning year should start

The handover leaves open whether the planning period is 2027 or a rolling twelve months. Because the board is meeting in the second half of a calendar year, the answer has real consequences for both the numbers and the team's workload.

The recommendation

Run a foundation cycle now; start the first full V/TO year on 1 January 2027
  1. Foundation cycle — roughly 90 days from board approval. Run the seven recommended Rocks. Deliberately light on new initiatives, heavy on definitions, data, and holiday execution. Weekly leadership meeting starts immediately and does not stop.
  2. Annual planning session — December. By then the company holds three months of clean financials, a complete 2026 wholesale picture, a supply plan, a real average-ticket trend, and a full-year 2026 actual. Set the 2027 numbers against that, not against an unresolved run rate.
  3. First full V/TO planning year — 1 January to 31 December 2027. Four clean quarters, aligned to the accounting year, the tax year, and the compliance calendar.

Why not simply set a 12-month goal starting now

  • A rolling twelve months starting mid-year straddles two calendar years, which means the revenue goal will never tie cleanly to any financial statement, tax filing, or the confirmed 2025 baseline the board is being asked to compare against.
  • The 2026 run rate is currently indeterminate. Setting a twelve-month number now means setting it on top of the one unresolved fact that matters most.
  • The first cycle lands on the holiday season. Loading a lean team with a full annual plan and a heavy Rock set in the same weeks it earns a large share of its carry-out revenue is a predictable way to achieve neither.
The counter-argument, stated fairly

Waiting until January can be read as losing momentum, and momentum is genuinely fragile after a board approves a new system. The mitigation is that the foundation cycle is not a pause — the weekly meeting, the dashboard, the seven Rocks, and the holiday revenue push all begin immediately. What waits is only the annual number, and only until there is a defensible basis for it.
